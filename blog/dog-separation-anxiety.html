The Dog Who Can't Be Left Alone

Separation anxiety is a panic attack, not a protest. That one distinction changes everything about how you treat it.

You leave for twenty minutes. You come back to a scratched door, a puddle by the mat, and a neighbour mentioning the howling again.

Then someone tells you the dog is being spiteful, or trying to be the boss, or needs to "learn". None of that is true, and acting on it makes things worse.

Anxiety or boredom?

They look similar and need opposite plans. Set a camera on your phone and watch the first thirty minutes.

  • Anxiety: distress starts within minutes of the door closing. Pacing, panting, drooling, howling, scratching at the exit specifically. Food is refused. The dog does not settle at all.
  • Boredom: the dog settles first, then gets up later and finds something to destroy — usually something fun, not the door. Food gets eaten.

Boredom is solved with enrichment and exercise. Anxiety is not, and no amount of a longer walk will touch it.

Rule out pain and the bladder first

Sudden-onset separation problems in an adult dog deserve a vet visit before any training. Pain, urinary infections and age-related cognitive change all show up this way, and training a medical problem is a waste of everyone's time.

Why the popular advice backfires

  • "Ignore them completely before you leave." Cold departures do not reduce panic; they just remove the reassurance. There is no evidence this helps.
  • "Let them cry it out." Flooding a panicking animal does not teach calm. It teaches that panic is survivable and usually deepens it.
  • "Get another dog for company." Sometimes helps, often just gives you two dogs with a problem. Never do this as a treatment.
  • "Crate them." For a dog panicking about confinement, a crate can turn distress into injury.

What actually works: stay under the threshold

The method is unglamorous. You work out how long your dog can be alone before panic starts — sometimes ten seconds — and you never exceed it. Then you extend by tiny amounts, hundreds of times.

  1. Break the departure cues. Pick up your keys and sit down. Put your coat on and make tea. Do this until the cues stop predicting anything.
  2. Start absurdly small. Step outside, close the door, come straight back in. No fuss either way.
  3. Build unevenly. Five seconds, twenty, ten, forty, fifteen. Random lengths stop your dog counting down.
  4. Watch the camera, not the clock. The dog decides the pace. If you see the first ear flick of worry, you went too far — drop back.
  5. Do not leave them longer than they can cope with in the meantime. Day care, a sitter, a friend, work in the car park — whatever it takes. Every panicked absence undoes days of work.

How long

Weeks to months, and progress is not linear — a good fortnight is often followed by a bad week for no visible reason. It is slow, but it is one of the problems where careful owners get genuinely excellent outcomes.

If your dog is injuring themselves trying to escape, speak to your vet as well as a trainer. Medication alongside a behaviour plan is sometimes the kindest and fastest route, and there is nothing to feel bad about in that.
