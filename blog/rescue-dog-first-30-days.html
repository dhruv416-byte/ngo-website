The First 30 Days With a Rescue Dog

The dog you bring home is not the dog you'll have in a month. Here's what to expect, week by week.

Rescue adoptions rarely fail in week one. They fail in week three, when the quiet, polite dog who arrived starts barking at the postman, guarding the sofa, or pulling like a train — and the family assumes they've been misled.

Nobody misled you. This is simply what settling in looks like.

Week one: do almost nothing

Your new dog has just been through kennels, a car journey, and a house full of unfamiliar smells and people. They are not ready to meet your friends, visit the park or be tested on their recall.

Week one is for decompression:

  • Short, quiet lead walks — five or ten minutes, somewhere boring. Sniffing is the point.
  • No visitors. None. It can wait a fortnight.
  • A safe spot — a bed in a low-traffic corner or an open crate — that nobody, especially children, ever disturbs.
  • Let them approach you rather than the other way round. Sit on the floor, look away, don't reach over their head.

A dog that sleeps a lot this week is a dog that's coping well.

Week two: routine, not training

Dogs settle on predictability far more than on affection. Feed, walk and sleep at roughly the same times each day, and your dog starts to relax because the day becomes guessable.

Start basic house habits now — where to sleep, what's off limits, going outside for the toilet. Assume no house training at all, even for an adult dog, and take them out as often as you would a puppy. Many rescues were never taught, or were kennelled long enough to forget.

Week three: the dog wakes up

This is the week the calls come in. The dog who was perfect suddenly has opinions — barking at noises, resource guarding a chew, pulling on the lead, testing what happens if they ignore you.

This is good news, even though it doesn't feel like it. A shut-down dog is a stressed dog. Behaviour appearing means they feel safe enough to be themselves. It's also the real starting point of training, because now you can see what you're actually working with.

Two things to hold onto here: don't punish what surfaces, and get help early rather than after three bad months.

Week four: start teaching

Now the foundations go in — name response, coming when called on a long line, settling on a mat, walking without pulling. Short sessions, five minutes at a time, all reward-based.

If your dog has a history you don't know, assume nothing. Test nothing. Some rescues are frightened of brooms, or men in high-vis, or the stairs. You find out gently, and you work around it rather than through it.

The things people get wrong

  • Too much, too soon. The busy park on day two is where reactivity gets built.
  • Off lead too early. Use a long line for at least the first month. A rescue that bolts in an unfamiliar area can run for miles.
  • Comforting is "rewarding fear". It isn't. You cannot reinforce an emotion. If your dog is frightened, comfort them.
  • Skipping the vet. Sudden grumpiness is often pain — ears, teeth, hips. Rule it out before you call it behaviour.
  • Expecting gratitude. Your dog doesn't know they've been rescued. They know the world changed again. Trust is earned over months.

The rule of thumb

Three days to stop panicking. Three weeks to settle into the routine. Three months to feel like your dog.

It's rough, but it's close, and it stops people giving up in week three — which is the single most useful thing this whole guide can do.
