Why Your Dog Lunges on the Lead (and What Actually Helps)

It looks like aggression. It's almost always fear. And the usual advice makes it worse.

You see another dog at the end of the road. Your stomach drops. You shorten the lead, your dog stiffens, and by the time the other dog is level with you there's barking, lunging, and a stranger giving you a look.

This is lead reactivity, and it's one of the most common reasons families contact us. It is also one of the most misunderstood.

Your dog is not being dominant

The "he's trying to be the boss" explanation has been out of date for decades. A reactive dog is almost always doing one of three things:

  • Frightened — the other dog is too close and they want distance.
  • Frustrated — they desperately want to say hello and the lead won't let them.
  • Sore — pain makes dogs snappy. Hips, ears and teeth are worth a vet check before any training.

All three look identical from the other end of the lead. Only the first two are training problems.

Why the lead makes it worse

Off-lead, a worried dog has options: move away, curve around, sniff the ground and pretend they haven't noticed. On a lead, all of that is taken away. They can't get distance, so they do the only thing left — make a lot of noise and hope the scary thing goes away.

And it works. The other dog does pass. From your dog's point of view, barking succeeded. That's why it gets louder over the months rather than fading out.

The distance rule

Every reactive dog has a distance at which they can see another dog and still think. Fifty metres, maybe. Twenty. Sometimes across a whole field.

Inside that distance, your dog is over threshold — the thinking part of the brain is offline and no amount of treats, commands or "leave it" will land. Outside it, they can learn.

Almost all real progress comes from working just outside that line, and from protecting it fiercely. Nothing is gained by pushing a dog through a meltdown.

What to actually do on the walk

  1. Spot the other dog first. This is the whole skill. Scan ahead; you want to see them before your dog does.
  2. Create distance immediately. Cross the road, step behind a parked car, turn down a side street, walk into a driveway. This is not failure — it's the training.
  3. Feed while the other dog is visible. Something excellent: chicken, cheese, hot dog. Not dry kibble. Feed steadily the whole time the other dog is in view, and stop the second they're gone.
  4. Let your dog look. Don't force them to stare at you. Seeing the other dog and then choosing to look back at you is exactly the behaviour you want.
  5. Leave before it's too much. One calm pass is a better session than ten minutes of struggling.

Repeated over weeks, this changes what another dog means. Other dog appears → chicken appears. It sounds too simple. It's the most reliable thing we do.

What to stop doing

  • Tightening the lead as you approach. Your dog feels that tension travel down the lead before they've even seen anything. Keep your arm loose.
  • Telling them off. Punishing a frightened dog for growling teaches them to skip the growl next time. The warning disappears; the bite doesn't.
  • Shock, prong or spray collars. They pair pain with the sight of other dogs — you are building the exact fear you're trying to fix.
  • "Just let them meet, they'll sort it out." Forced greetings between two tense dogs on tight leads are how the worst incidents start.
  • Flooding the busy park. Exposure without distance isn't practice, it's a bad day repeated.

Kit that helps

A well-fitted Y-shaped harness and a two-metre lead. Not a retractable one — you can't control distance with a flexi, and that's the only thing you need control of. If your dog is very strong, a double-ended lead clipped front and back gives you steering without pain.

How long does it take?

Honest answer: months, not weeks — and the curve isn't smooth. There will be a brilliant fortnight followed by a terrible Tuesday when a spaniel appears from behind a wheelie bin. That's not you undoing your work. That's a bad day.

Judge progress over a month, not a walk.
